The West DeLand Residential Historic District lies west of downtown, roughly bounded by University, Florida, New York and Orange Avenues. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places on November 20, 1992, and the listing counts 375 historic buildings. The City of DeLand names it as one of four historic districts, alongside the downtown, Stetson University and Garden districts.

What an older home changes about water damage

Age alone does not tell us which pipes, roof or foundation a particular house has, and many homes here have been updated over the years. What changes is the material at risk. Original plaster, wood floors and trim cost more to replace than modern finishes, so the goal is often to dry what can be saved before deciding what must come out.

Tell the line what your house has, such as plaster walls, hardwood floors or a raised foundation with a crawl space. Those answers shape how drying is planned. If you can see under the house, mention whether the ground there is wet.

Check before replacing exterior features

Whether a repair needs city review depends on whether your property is on the local historic register or inside a designated district. The City of DeLand land development regulations cover historic districts and landmarks, so ask the City Planning Department before replacing windows, siding or other exterior parts. Interior drying and cleanup normally do not change the outside of a building, but confirm if you are unsure.

Take photographs of original materials before any are removed, and ask that salvageable trim and fixtures be stored rather than discarded. Those photographs also support an insurance record.

Safety steps for an older home

Shut off the main water valve if a pipe is leaking and the valve is easy and safe to reach.

Do not stand in water near outlets, and call an electrician if the panel is wet.

Older wiring and finishes may contain materials that should not be disturbed, so avoid sanding or scraping wet walls before they are assessed.

Extraction and drying in a historic house

A crew removes standing water first, then measures moisture in walls, floors and framing. Structural drying uses air movers and dehumidifiers to pull moisture out of materials that can be saved. Moisture readings repeat until the building reaches a normal range, and the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard describes how that drying should be measured and recorded.

Water from a sewage backup or from outdoors is contaminated, and porous materials that touched it are often removed. That is a safety decision for the crew, and it is not a judgment about the age of your house.

Flood maps and insurance for older homes

Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status is not verified for this page. Zones can differ from one block to the next, so use FEMA's Flood Map Service Center to look up the exact property.

Policies differ in how they pay for repairs to older materials, so ask your agent how yours treats plaster, wood floors and trim. Keep photos, receipts and the drying readings in one folder.

Questions we hear in West DeLand Historic District

Is my house inside the West DeLand Historic District?

The district is roughly bounded by University, Florida, New York and Orange Avenues. For a definite answer, ask the City of DeLand Planning Department, which keeps the historic resource maps.

Can plaster walls be dried instead of replaced?

Sometimes. It depends on how long the plaster stayed wet, whether it has loosened from the lath and whether the water was clean. A crew can check with a moisture meter before anything is removed.

Do I need city approval before drying my house?

Interior drying normally does not change the outside of a building. Replacing windows, siding or other exterior features may need review, so ask the City Planning Department first.

Will insurance replace original materials?

That depends on your policy wording and the cause of the loss. Ask your agent before work starts how the policy treats older finishes, and keep photographs of the originals.
