Water in a business means lost time and lost stock. Crews extract, dry and coordinate with owners, tenants and adjusters so the doors can reopen.

Water in a commercial building is an emergency, so call the 24/7 line, shut the main water valve if you can and move inventory, files and electronics off the floor. Cut power to wet areas only from a dry panel and stay clear of any equipment in standing water. Photograph the damage before anything moves, because your insurer will want the record.

Commercial water damage restoration covers the extraction, drying and repair of businesses, including offices, restaurants, retail shops, medical suites and multi-tenant buildings. These jobs differ from homes because several parties are involved, and every day closed costs money. The goal is a dry, safe building and the paperwork to support a claim.

When a business water loss is an emergency

Call right away when water reaches inventory, electrical panels, server rooms or floors where customers walk. Same-day response is offered when available for active leaks, and after-hours calls get the same questions so a crew can prepare. Tell the line if the building is multi-tenant, since water often crosses into the unit next door.

Electrical, slip and contamination hazards

Do not enter areas with water at electrical gear. Call the power utility or 911 for sparks, burning smells or gas.

Wet floors in customer areas are a slip risk. Block off the space and keep staff out of contaminated water, such as sewage or kitchen grease.

Why Deland buildings take on water

Downtown Deland, along Woodland Boulevard, has older commercial buildings with shared walls and roofs, and a leak in one unit can move to the next. Flat or low-slope roofs hold water after a storm and test aging flashing. Walk the roof drains each spring so a summer cloudburst does not find them clogged.

Stetson University, the Deland Municipal Airport and the Volusia County Fair and Expo Center bring large buildings and events to the area, and big roofs and long plumbing runs mean bigger losses. Sprinkler lines, rooftop HVAC drains and restroom supply lines are the usual culprits. A maintenance walk-through after each major storm catches issues before tenants do.

Restaurants and salons add their own risks. Dishwasher lines, grease traps and sink drains fail at the worst times, and wet back-of-house floors can mean a health inspector visit. Keep a water shutoff location map on file for each space.

How crews handle a commercial loss

Crews assess the whole building, not only the visible wet areas. Moisture meters and thermal imaging find water behind walls and under floors, and extraction units remove standing water. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers dry the structure, with readings logged for each space.

Contents are protected where possible. Inventory, files and equipment are moved or elevated, and we document what was affected. The structural drying page explains the drying phase in more detail.

Insurance and commercial losses

Commercial policies vary widely and may include business interruption coverage or flood exclusions. Read yours and call your agent early. Florida Statute 627.70132 covers notice of property claims within one year of the loss.

Landlords and tenants should check who insures the building and who insures contents before a loss. Clear records and photos help in either case.

The industry standard behind the work

Professional water damage restoration follows a published industry document, the ANSI/IICRC S500. It sets out how a loss is assessed, how affected materials are classified and how drying or cleanup is documented.

Ask any company how it applies that standard on your job. A clear answer should cover readings taken, equipment placed and the records you will receive. This page describes the approach, and it is not a claim about any individual certificate.
